weather term

Dew Point

The temperature at which air becomes saturated with moisture and water vapour starts to condense into dew, fog or clouds.

Unlike relative humidity, dew point is an absolute measure of how much moisture is actually in the air, which makes it a more reliable way to judge how muggy a day will feel. A dew point below about 10°C feels comfortably dry, while anything above 20°C usually feels noticeably sticky regardless of the air temperature itself. When the air temperature drops to match the dew point overnight, moisture condenses out as dew, or as fog if it happens near the ground.

How does dew point feel? Comfort chart

The dew point is the best single number for how humid it feels outside. These ranges are a widely used rule of thumb; people used to a humid climate may feel comfortable at slightly higher values.

Dew point vs. relative humidity

Relative humidity tells you how close the air is to saturation at its current temperature, so it changes during the day even when the amount of moisture stays the same: it is usually highest at dawn and lowest in the afternoon. The dew point does not depend on the temperature in that way, so it is a better guide to how muggy it will feel. A summer afternoon at 32°C (90°F) and 40% relative humidity has a dew point of about 17°C (62°F) and feels noticeably humid, while a cool morning at 10°C (50°F) and 90% humidity has a dew point of only about 8°C (47°F) and feels fresh.

How to calculate the dew point

The dew point can be calculated from the temperature and the relative humidity with the Magnus formula. With the temperature T in °C and the relative humidity RH in percent, first calculate γ = ln(RH/100) + 17.62 × T / (243.12 + T); the dew point is then 243.12 × γ / (17.62 − γ). For example, at 25°C (77°F) and 60% relative humidity, γ is about 1.13 and the dew point is about 16.7°C (62°F).

For a quick estimate there is a simple rule of thumb that works well above about 50% humidity: subtract (100 − RH) / 5 from the temperature in °C. At 25°C and 60% humidity that gives 25 − 8 = 17°C, close to the exact answer.

In Miami and Houston almost every July day is oppressive, with a dew point above 21°C (70°F), while in Phoenix the air is much drier despite the heat. That is why 100°F feels very different in Phoenix and in Houston: in dry air, sweat evaporates easily and cools the body, in humid air it does not.

Dew, frost and fog

When the air near the ground cools down to its dew point, water vapor condenses. On grass and car windows this forms dew. If the dew point is below freezing, the moisture turns straight into ice crystals and you get frost; the dew point is then also called the frost point. When the air itself cools to its dew point, tiny droplets form in the air and you get fog. The smaller the gap between the temperature and the dew point in the evening, the more likely fog is by the next morning.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good dew point?
For most people a dew point below about 16°C (60°F) feels comfortable. Above 18°C (65°F) it starts to feel muggy, and above 21°C (70°F) oppressive.

Is dew point the same as humidity?
No. Relative humidity is a percentage that changes with the temperature during the day. The dew point is a temperature that shows how much moisture is actually in the air, and is a better guide to how humid it feels.

Can the dew point be higher than the temperature?
No. The dew point can at most equal the air temperature. When they are equal, the relative humidity is 100% and the air is saturated, which often means fog, dew or rain.

What is the highest dew point ever recorded?
One of the highest dew points on record is 35°C (95°F), reported in Dhahran, Saudi Arabia, on July 8, 2003, where the heat index reached about 81°C (178°F).
