weather term

Jet Stream

A narrow band of fast-moving air high in the atmosphere that steers weather systems across the mid-latitudes.

Jet streams form where large temperature differences meet, typically between polar and tropical air masses, and can reach speeds well over 200 km/h. The position and strength of the jet stream has a huge influence on day-to-day weather: a jet stream that dips further south than usual can drag colder air with it, while a jet stream running further north often means a milder, wetter pattern for places in its southern flank.

Where and how fast?

The jet stream is a narrow band of very strong wind high in the atmosphere, near the top of the troposphere, at about 9 to 12 kilometers (30,000 to 39,000 feet), around the cruising altitude of passenger jets. Wind speeds in the core are often 100 to 200 mph (160 to 320 km/h) and can be higher in winter. The jet stream blows mainly from west to east, but in a wavy, meandering pattern rather than a straight line.

Why is there a jet stream?

The jet stream forms where cold polar air meets warmer air from further south. The bigger the temperature contrast, the stronger the jet. That is why it is strongest in winter, when the difference between the frozen Arctic and the warmer south is largest, and why it shifts north in summer. There are two main jet streams in each hemisphere: the polar jet, which matters most for the weather in the US, Canada and Europe, and the subtropical jet, further south and higher up.

How the jet stream steers your weather

The jet stream acts as a steering current for weather systems. Low-pressure systems form and strengthen along it and follow its path, so where the jet stream runs, storms run. When it dips south over a region, in what forecasters call a trough, cold air pours in behind it and the weather turns unsettled. When it bulges north, in a ridge, warm air flows in and the weather is often settled. If the pattern gets stuck, the same weather can last for days or weeks: a stalled ridge can bring a heat wave, a stalled trough a long run of rain or cold.

El Niño and the jet stream

During El Niño the subtropical jet stream over the Pacific is often stronger and reaches further east, steering more storms across the southern US. That is one reason El Niño winters tend to be wetter in the South and milder in the North; read more in our winter 2026-27 forecast.

The jet stream and flights

Airlines plan routes around the jet stream. Flying east with a strong tailwind saves time and fuel, while flying west into it takes longer. That is why a flight from New York to London is usually shorter than the return flight. In February 2020, a British Airways Boeing 747 crossed from New York to London in under five hours, helped by a jet stream of more than 200 mph during Storm Ciara.

Frequently asked questions

How high is the jet stream?
About 9 to 12 kilometers (30,000 to 39,000 feet) above the ground, near the top of the troposphere.

How fast is the jet stream?
The core is often 100 to 200 mph (160 to 320 km/h), and in winter it can be even faster.

Why does the jet stream move?
It follows the boundary between cold polar air and warmer air. As that boundary shifts with the seasons and with weather systems, the jet stream moves north and south and develops large waves.
