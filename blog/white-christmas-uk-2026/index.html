White Christmas 2026 in the UK: What Are the Odds?

By Emma Clarke · November 20, 2026

A white Christmas is a classic British wish, and a popular bet. But what actually counts as one? By the official definition, a single snowflake is enough, which makes a white Christmas surprisingly common. A Christmas morning with snow on the ground is a very different story. Here are the odds, based on Met Office records and 36 years of data for London, Edinburgh and Dublin.

Key facts
  • Met Office definition: a single snowflake observed falling anywhere in the UK during the 24 hours of December 25 counts as a white Christmas.
  • Snow falling: since 1960, around half of the years have seen at least 5% of Met Office stations record snow falling on Christmas Day.
  • Snow on the ground: widespread snow cover on Christmas morning happened only four times since 1960: in 1981, 1995, 2009 and 2010.
  • London: in our data, there was snow on the ground on Christmas morning in only 2 of the last 36 years, in 2009 and 2010.
  • Edinburgh: the best odds of our three cities, with snow on the ground in 7 of 36 years.

What counts as a white Christmas in the UK?

The Met Office definition is simple: a white Christmas means a single snowflake observed falling in the 24 hours of December 25, somewhere in the UK. Traditionally, one location was used, the Met Office building in London, but with the rise of betting on a white Christmas, more locations have been added. Snow doesn't have to settle. That is very different from the US, where a white Christmas means at least an inch of snow on the ground.

How often is it a white Christmas?

Because a single snowflake anywhere in the country is enough, a "technical" white Christmas is fairly common. Since 1960, around half of the years have seen at least 5% of Met Office stations record snow falling on Christmas Day. In recent years, 2020 and 2021 both had snow falling at 6% of stations, 2022 at 9% and 2023 at 11%, but none of those years had widespread snow on the ground.

Snow lying on the ground on Christmas morning is much rarer. The Met Office counts a Christmas as having widespread snow cover when more than 40% of stations report snow on the ground at 9 a.m. That has happened only four times since 1960: in 1981, 1995, 2009 and 2010. In 2010, snow lay at 83% of stations, the highest share ever recorded.

In London, snow lay on the ground on Christmas morning only in 2009 and 2010, the two famous snowy Christmases of recent decades. Edinburgh, further north and colder, did better, with snow on the ground in 7 of 36 years. Dublin, close to the relatively mild Irish Sea, had snow cover in just 2 years. More often, Christmas Day in all three cities is mild and grey, with highs around 6 to 8°C (43 to 46°F) and rain in roughly 4 of every 10 years.

Will it snow this Christmas?

Nobody can say that in November. Whether snow falls on December 25 depends on the weather pattern in the days just before Christmas, which only becomes predictable about two weeks ahead. From around December 11, check the 14-day forecast for your city; a cold northerly or easterly flow in the forecast is the first hint that a white Christmas could be on the cards. Frequently asked questions

What counts as a white Christmas in the UK?
According to the Met Office, a single snowflake observed falling in the 24 hours of December 25, somewhere in the UK. Snow doesn't have to settle on the ground.

When was the last white Christmas in the UK?
By the Met Office definition, recent years such as 2020 to 2023 counted, with snow falling at 6 to 11% of stations. The last Christmas with widespread snow on the ground was 2010, when snow lay at 83% of stations.

How often is there snow on the ground at Christmas in London?
Rarely. In our data for 1990-2025, there was snow on the ground in London on Christmas morning only in 2009 and 2010.

Where in the UK is a white Christmas most likely?
In the north and on higher ground, especially in Scotland. In our data, Edinburgh had snow on the ground on Christmas morning far more often than London or Dublin.
