What Is Lake-Effect Snow? The Great Lakes Snow Explained

By Emma Clarke · November 10, 2026

Every late fall and winter, towns downwind of the Great Lakes brace for a kind of snow most of the country never sees. Lake-effect snow can pile up several inches an hour, close highways and bury one neighborhood while the sun shines a few miles away. How does a lake make snow, why are some places hit so much harder than others, and what should you do when a lake-effect warning goes up?

Image: GOES East satellite view of lake-effect snow bands over the Great Lakes, NOAA/NESDIS, Wikimedia Commons (public domain)

Key facts
  • What it is: snow that forms when very cold air moves over the relatively warm, open water of a large lake, picks up heat and moisture, and drops it as snow on the downwind shore.
  • Where: the snowbelts east and south of the Great Lakes, from western Michigan to Cleveland, Erie, Buffalo, the Tug Hill Plateau and Syracuse.
  • Peak season: November to January, when the lakes are still warm and mostly free of ice, while the air above them is already wintry.
  • Very local: lake-effect snow often falls in narrow bands, so one town can get several feet while a town 10 miles away stays almost dry.
  • Warnings: the National Weather Service issues Lake Effect Snow Watches and Warnings for the areas around the Great Lakes.

What is lake-effect snow?

Lake-effect snow is snow made by a lake. It happens when cold air, usually from Canada, blows across a large body of relatively warm, unfrozen water. The lake warms the lowest layer of air and adds moisture to it. That warm, moist air rises, cools and forms clouds, and as the air reaches the far shore it drops the moisture as snow. The Great Lakes are the best-known example in the world, but the same process happens over other large lakes and even over seas, such as the Great Salt Lake in Utah.

Unlike a big winter storm, lake-effect snow does not need a low-pressure system passing overhead. It often starts on the cold, clear side of a storm, after a cold front has passed and a steady wind brings in Arctic air.

How does lake-effect snow form?

Three things matter most. The first is the temperature difference between the lake and the air above it. A common rule of thumb used by forecasters is that the air about 5,000 feet (1,500 m) up should be at least about 23°F (13°C) colder than the lake surface. The bigger the difference, the more the air rises and the heavier the snow can be.

The second is the fetch: the distance the wind travels over open water. A wind that blows along the length of a lake picks up far more heat and moisture than one that crosses it at its narrowest point. That is why the wind direction decides which towns get the heaviest snow.

The third is the shape of the land downwind. When the snow-laden air reaches the shore, friction slows it down and hills force it to rise, squeezing out even more snow. Higher ground just inland from the lakes, such as the Tug Hill Plateau east of Lake Ontario and the hills south of Buffalo and east of Cleveland, often gets the most.

Snowbelts: why one town gets feet and the next gets nothing

Lake-effect snow usually falls in long, narrow bands, sometimes only 5 to 20 miles wide, lined up with the wind. Under a strong single band, snow can fall at several inches per hour, with thunder and lightning at times. Just outside the band, skies can be partly sunny. If the wind direction holds steady for hours, the band stays over the same towns and the snow piles up there. If the wind shifts, the band moves, and a different set of towns gets hit.

Over many winters, this creates snowbelts: areas on the east and south shores of the lakes that are much snowier than places upwind. The main ones are western Michigan along Lake Michigan, northwest Indiana, the Upper Peninsula of Michigan along Lake Superior, northeast Ohio east of Cleveland, northwest Pennsylvania around Erie, western New York south of Buffalo, and the Tug Hill Plateau and Syracuse area east of Lake Ontario. Tug Hill is one of the snowiest places in the eastern United States. On the upwind side of the same lakes, cities such as Chicago and Toronto see lake-effect snow far less often, only when the wind blows off the lake toward them.

Why November to January is the peak

Water warms and cools much more slowly than land. By November, the air over the Great Lakes region can already be wintry, while the lakes still hold much of their summer heat. That big contrast is exactly what lake-effect snow needs, so the heaviest events often come in late fall and early winter.

As winter goes on, the lakes cool down and the contrast shrinks. Ice also starts to form, and ice cuts off the supply of heat and moisture. Lake Erie, the shallowest of the Great Lakes, cools fastest and often freezes over for much of its area in a cold winter, which largely ends the lake-effect season for the Erie and Buffalo snowbelts. Lake Ontario is deeper and rarely freezes over completely, so lake-effect snow can continue east of it all winter. Famous lake-effect snowstorms

Buffalo is the city most often linked with lake-effect snow, and two November storms show how extreme it can get. In November 2014, a band off Lake Erie stalled over the southern suburbs of Buffalo for days and dumped up to about 7 feet of snow in some towns, while the north side of the city got only a few inches. Drivers were stranded on roads, including the New York State Thruway.

In November 2022, another Lake Erie band set up south of Buffalo, and towns around Orchard Park, home of the Buffalo Bills, got more than 6 feet. The Bills had to move their home game that weekend to Detroit. In both storms, the snow fell in a narrow strip, a classic example of how local lake-effect snow can be.

How forecasters warn, and how to stay safe

The National Weather Service offices around the Great Lakes issue a Lake Effect Snow Watch when heavy lake-effect snow is possible in the coming days, and a Lake Effect Snow Warning when it is expected or already happening. The exact criteria vary by office. Because the bands are so narrow, the warnings often name specific counties, and forecasters update them as the wind shifts. Lighter events fall under a Winter Weather Advisory. See what the warning colors mean in our guide to weather warnings.

The biggest danger is on the road. Driving into a band can take you from dry pavement to whiteout visibility in minutes. If a warning is out, avoid travel through the band if you can, and if you must drive, slow down, keep your lights on and carry a winter kit with a blanket, water, a phone charger and a shovel. At home, clear snow from vents and roofs where it is safe, and check on neighbors.

Frequently asked questions

What is lake-effect snow?
Snow that forms when very cold air moves over the relatively warm, open water of a large lake, picks up heat and moisture, and drops it as snow on the downwind shore.

Why does lake-effect snow fall in such narrow bands?
The clouds line up with the wind as it blows across the lake. If the wind direction stays steady, a band can sit over the same towns for hours, while places a few miles away stay almost dry.

When is lake-effect snow season?
Mostly from November to January, when the lakes are still warm and free of ice. Once a lake freezes over, lake-effect snow downwind of it largely stops. Lake Ontario rarely freezes over, so its snowbelts can get lake-effect snow all winter.

Which cities get the most lake-effect snow?
The snowbelts on the east and south shores of the Great Lakes, including the Buffalo and Erie areas, northeast Ohio east of Cleveland, Syracuse and the Tug Hill Plateau in New York, and western Michigan.
