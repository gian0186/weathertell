What Does "Feels Like" Temperature Actually Mean?

By Emma Clarke · July 2, 2026

The number on the thermometer isn't always what your body actually experiences. Wind, humidity and sunshine can all shift how a temperature feels, sometimes by a surprising amount.

Wind makes cold air feel colder

When it's windy, your body loses heat to the surrounding air faster than it would on a still day, because the wind constantly sweeps away the thin layer of air your skin has warmed. This is what's known as wind chill, and it can make a genuinely mild-looking temperature feel several degrees colder than the thermometer suggests. It's a big part of why a calm 5°C day can feel comfortable while a windy 8°C day feels bitter. Meteorologists calculate wind chill using a formula that combines air temperature with wind speed at roughly head height, since that combination best matches how quickly exposed skin actually loses heat. As a rough real-world example, a 5°C day with only a light breeze might still feel close to 5°C, while the same 5°C with a stiff 30 km/h wind can feel closer to 0°C or below — a meaningful difference for how many layers you actually need, not just a rounding error.

Humidity and sunshine matter too

On a still, sunny day, direct sunlight can make the air feel warmer than the official reading, since your skin absorbs radiant heat on top of the surrounding air temperature. In hot, humid weather, the opposite effect makes things worse: your body cools itself by sweating, but in humid air that sweat evaporates more slowly, so heat builds up rather than escaping. That's why a humid 30°C day can feel far more oppressive than a dry 34°C one. The equivalent calculation for hot weather is sometimes called the heat index, and it works in the opposite direction to wind chill: rising humidity pushes the felt temperature up rather than down. In very humid, windless conditions, the heat index can run five degrees or more above the actual air temperature, which is part of why tropical and subtropical cities often feel more draining than a drier location showing the same number on the thermometer.

Use "feels like" for your outdoor plans

When you're cycling, walking or exercising outside, look at the combination of temperature, wind and precipitation rather than the raw number alone. Choose clothing for what you'll actually feel, not just for what the number next to the thermometer icon says. Someone cycling hard into the wind experiences that same wind chill very differently from someone standing still at a bus stop. A useful habit is to check the feels-like figure alongside the wind speed before deciding what to wear, rather than glancing at the temperature alone and assuming that settles it. Layered clothing tends to work better than one thick layer in changeable, windy conditions, since you can adjust as effort and exposure change through the activity, and a windproof outer layer often makes more difference than an extra insulating one underneath.

Why does it vary from person to person?

Feels-like temperature is a calculated estimate, not a personal reading — it doesn't know how hard you're working, what you're wearing, or how sensitive you personally are to cold or heat. Effort, clothing, shade and individual tolerance all shape how a given set of conditions actually feels to you. Two people standing in the same spot can genuinely disagree about how cold it is, and both can be right. Age, fitness and how acclimatised someone is to a particular climate all play a role too — someone used to a cold climate may find a given temperature far more comfortable than a visitor from somewhere warmer, and vice versa. None of that makes the feels-like figure useless; it's simply an average estimate for a typical adult in typical clothing, meant as a starting point rather than the final word for every individual.

Frequently asked questions

Is "feels like" temperature a real, measured temperature?
No. It's a calculated estimate of how conditions such as wind and humidity are likely to feel, not a direct measurement in its own right.

What should I pay attention to in cold, windy weather?
Wind and feels-like temperature usually matter more than the raw air temperature. Layered clothing generally works better than one thick layer, since you can adjust as conditions change.

Does feels-like temperature account for sunshine?
Only partially. Most feels-like calculations focus on wind and humidity; direct sunshine can make a still, sunny day feel warmer than either the air temperature or the calculated feels-like figure suggests, especially for anyone standing in direct light rather than shade.
