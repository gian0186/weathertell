What Is an Atmospheric River? Rivers in the Sky Explained

By Emma Clarke · November 10, 2026

In fall and winter, forecasters on the West Coast often warn of an "atmospheric river" heading for the coast. Right now, in mid-November 2026, the Pacific Northwest and British Columbia are again being hit by a series of them. The term sounds dramatic, but it describes something very real: a narrow stream of moist air that can drop days of heavy rain and mountain snow in one place. Here is what an atmospheric river is, how strong it can get, and why the same storm can be a blessing and a disaster.

Image: GOES satellite view of an atmospheric river approaching the Pacific Northwest, December 4, 2023, CSU/CIRA and NOAA, Wikimedia Commons (public domain)

Key facts
  • What it is: a long, narrow band in the atmosphere that carries large amounts of water vapor, often from the tropics toward the mid-latitudes. NOAA calls them "rivers in the sky".
  • Pineapple Express: a well-known type of atmospheric river that carries moisture from the waters near Hawaii to the US West Coast and British Columbia.
  • Where they hit: most often the West Coast of North America, from California to British Columbia, but also western Europe and the UK.
  • AR scale: scientists at Scripps (CW3E) rank atmospheric rivers from AR 1 (weak) to AR 5 (exceptional), based on how much moisture they carry and how long they last.
  • Good and bad: they refill reservoirs and build mountain snowpack, but strong or slow-moving ones cause flooding and landslides.

What is an atmospheric river?

NOAA describes atmospheric rivers as relatively long, narrow regions in the atmosphere, like rivers in the sky, that transport most of the water vapor outside of the tropics. They are typically hundreds of miles wide but can stretch for well over a thousand miles, often reaching from the warm subtropical ocean toward the mid-latitudes. They usually travel with a low-pressure system, along the strong winds ahead of its cold front, and are steered by the jet stream.

The amount of water is huge. According to NOAA, an average atmospheric river carries about as much water vapor as the average flow at the mouth of the Mississippi River. The vapor itself is invisible. It only becomes rain or snow when the moist air is forced to rise and cool, which is why the heaviest precipitation falls where an atmospheric river runs into mountains.

The Pineapple Express

The best-known atmospheric river has its own nickname: the Pineapple Express. It is a plume of moisture that starts in the tropical Pacific near Hawaii and flows northeast to the West Coast of North America. Because the air comes from the subtropics, it is warm as well as wet. That matters in winter: a Pineapple Express can bring rain high up into the mountains, falling on top of existing snow. Rain on snow melts the snowpack and sends even more water into rivers at once.

Not every atmospheric river is a Pineapple Express. Many draw their moisture from other parts of the Pacific, and some form over the Atlantic.

Where do atmospheric rivers hit?

The best-known target is the West Coast of North America. California, Oregon, Washington and British Columbia all get atmospheric rivers, mostly from fall to early spring. NOAA notes that they supply a large share of the West Coast's yearly precipitation, often 30 to 50 percent. When one stalls over the Coast Mountains or the Cascades, cities like Seattle and Vancouver can get several days of heavy rain, while the mountains get rain at low levels and deep snow higher up. Further south, San Francisco and Los Angeles get them less often, but often with bigger impacts on hillsides and rivers. During strong El Niño winters, the storm track often shifts south and can steer more of them toward California, as our winter 2026-27 forecast explains.

Atmospheric rivers also occur over the Atlantic. In western Europe and the UK, they are linked to many of the wettest winter storms. The flooding in Cumbria during Storm Desmond in December 2015, for example, was fed by an atmospheric river.

The AR scale: from 1 to 5

In 2019, scientists at the Center for Western Weather and Water Extremes (CW3E) at Scripps Institution of Oceanography introduced a scale to rank atmospheric rivers, much like hurricanes have categories. It looks at two things: how much water vapor the river carries, and how long it sits over one place. A longer event moves up a category, a short one moves down.

The five levels are AR 1 (weak), AR 2 (moderate), AR 3 (strong), AR 4 (extreme) and AR 5 (exceptional). The scale also says something about impacts. A weak AR 1 is mostly beneficial, bringing useful rain and snow. An AR 3 has a balance of benefits and hazards. An AR 5 is mostly hazardous, with a high risk of serious flooding. Forecasters on the West Coast now often mention the AR category when a big event is coming.

Water supply and floods: two sides of the same storm

For the western US, atmospheric rivers are essential. They fill reservoirs and build the mountain snowpack that melts in spring and summer and supplies water to farms and cities. A few strong atmospheric rivers can end a drought in a single winter.

The same storms are also the main cause of floods along the West Coast. Heavy rain for several days saturates the ground, rivers rise quickly, and steep slopes can give way in landslides and mudslides, especially on hillsides burned by recent wildfires. Two well-documented examples show both sides. In late December 2022 and early January 2023, a rapid series of atmospheric rivers hit California, causing widespread flooding and deadly impacts, but also helping to build one of the biggest Sierra Nevada snowpacks on record. In mid-November 2021, an atmospheric river brought extreme rain to southern British Columbia, causing floods and landslides that cut highways and rail lines to Vancouver, forced the evacuation of the city of Merritt and flooded farmland on the Sumas Prairie near Abbotsford.

How to stay safe during an atmospheric river

Follow your local National Weather Service office or Environment Canada, and take flood watches and warnings seriously. Never drive or walk through floodwater: just a foot (30 cm) of moving water can carry a car away. If you live below a steep slope or in a recent burn area, know your evacuation route and leave early if officials advise it. Watch for signs of a landslide, like new cracks, tilting trees or sudden muddy water in streams. Clear gutters and storm drains before the rain starts, and keep phones charged in case of power cuts from saturated ground and falling trees.

Frequently asked questions

What is an atmospheric river?
A long, narrow band in the atmosphere that carries large amounts of water vapor, often from the tropics toward the mid-latitudes. When it reaches land and is forced up over mountains, it can produce days of heavy rain and snow.

What is the Pineapple Express?
A type of atmospheric river that carries warm, moist air from the waters near Hawaii to the West Coast of North America. It often brings heavy rain and high snow levels in winter.

What do the AR 1 to AR 5 categories mean?
The CW3E scale ranks atmospheric rivers from AR 1 (weak) to AR 5 (exceptional), based on how much water vapor they carry and how long they last. Weak ones are mostly beneficial, exceptional ones mostly hazardous.

Are atmospheric rivers good or bad?
Both. They provide much of the West Coast's water and mountain snowpack, but strong or long-lasting atmospheric rivers cause flooding and landslides.
